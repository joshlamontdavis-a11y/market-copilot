# Market brief: An in-hive sensor that warns beekeepers when varroa mite levels are rising

Research date: 2026-10-28. Prices and competitors change, so check the links before relying on them.

Target customer: Hobbyist and small commercial (sideliner) beekeepers. Geography: United States. Depth: quick.

This brief prepares you for customer interviews. It does not show that anyone will buy.

Verified means the quoted text is on the cited page. Read each quote to judge whether it supports the claim.

## 1. Summary

Checked by check.py: 15 verified, 11 assumptions, 0 unverified.

### What we found

- **[Verified]** USDA's annual survey estimated the total number of US honey bee colonies at 2.71 million, stable from 2022 to 2023. (S12)
- **[Verified]** In that USDA survey, beekeepers reported Varroa mites among the leading stressors, affecting between 40 and 50 percent of colonies. (S13)
- **[Verified]** Hobbyists make up approximately 95% of US beekeepers but manage only 7% of colonies, per the 2022 National Colony Loss and Management survey. (S14)
- **[Verified]** An American Bee Journal columnist estimates about 1500 professional beekeepers and around 150 thousand hobby beekeepers in the US, calling the hobby figure a conservative guess. (S10)
- **[Assumption]** Hobbyists each keep only a few hives, so revenue per hobbyist customer at a per-hive price will be small.
- **[Verified]** A retailer of the BroodMinder-Cell hub states that this telemetry does not see varroa, foulbrood, or the state of the queen. (S3)
- **[Assumption]** The leading low-cost hive monitors do not measure mites, which leaves room for a product that does.
- **[Verified]** BroodMinder's maker recommends equipping 3 or 4 hives in an apiary of 30-50 for representative data. (S4)
- **[Assumption]** Small commercial beekeepers who follow this sampling practice would buy sensors for a few hives per yard, not every hive.
- **[Verified]** A beekeeping blog says complete monitoring setups including scales run $150-300 per hive, and that cost remains the primary barrier for small-scale beekeepers. (S16, S17)
- **[Verified]** Today's substitute is manual testing: mite counter jars and sticky boards sold to check mite levels. (S8, S9)
- **[Assumption]** No in-hive varroa sensor with a published US price turned up in this research; the closest product found (BeeRight) is based in Australia.

### What we still don't know

- Whether any sensor can estimate mite load accurately enough that beekeepers trust it over an alcohol wash or sugar roll.
- How often US hobbyists and sideliners actually test for mites today, and why they skip tests.
- How many sideliners (small commercial beekeepers) there are in the US; no federal count was found.
- How many hives a typical US hobbyist keeps; no sourced figure was found.
- BeeRight's price and whether it sells in the US.
- Current Arnia pricing; only dated, secondhand figures were found and none were read directly.
- The ApisProtect price and the $150-300 setup range come from one blog post whose dates are inconsistent; confirm with the vendors.
- Whether beekeepers prefer a one-time price or a subscription for alerts.

## 2. Riskiest assumptions

1. **A sensor sitting in the hive can estimate varroa levels reliably enough to replace or reduce manual mite tests.**
   - Why it's risky: If the reading isn't trusted, beekeepers will still do alcohol washes and the sensor adds cost without saving work. No product found proves this works at this price.
   - How to test it: Run a prototype in 5-10 hives next to biweekly alcohol washes for one season and compare the readings; separately ask beekeepers what accuracy they'd need, based on how they act on today's wash counts.
2. **Hobbyists will pay $150 per hive when a reusable mite test jar costs tens of dollars.**
   - Why it's risky: A blog source names cost as the main barrier for small-scale beekeepers, and $150 per hive multiplies across every hive.
   - How to test it: In interviews, ask what they spent on bees, gear, and mite treatment last year; then run a refundable pre-order page at $150/hive and measure conversion.
3. **Beekeepers lose colonies because they miss when mites rise, not because they don't treat or treat badly.**
   - Why it's risky: If the cause of loss is treatment choice or timing that they already know about, an early warning doesn't fix it.
   - How to test it: Ask 10 beekeepers to walk through their last lost colony and when they last tested before it died.
4. **Small commercial beekeepers will put a sensor in every hive rather than a few per yard.**
   - Why it's risky: BroodMinder's own guidance is to instrument 3-4 hives per 30-50, which caps units per sideliner.
   - How to test it: Ask sideliners how they decide which hives to sample for mites today and how many monitors they own now.

## 3. Interview script

Thanks for talking with me. I'm learning how beekeepers keep track of varroa mites. I'm not selling anything today.

1. Tell me about the last time you checked a hive for varroa mites.
   - Listen for: Method (wash, sugar roll, sticky board, none), how long it took, and how they felt about doing it.
2. How many times did you test for mites this past season, and what decided when you tested?
   - Listen for: Frequency, triggers (calendar, club advice, visible damage), and whether they skipped tests they meant to do.
3. Tell me about the last colony you lost. What do you think happened?
   - Listen for: Whether mites were the cause and whether an earlier warning would have changed what they did.
4. What did you do the last time a mite count came back high?
   - Listen for: How fast they acted, which treatment, and whether the problem is detection or action.
5. What did you spend on mite testing and treatment this year?
   - Listen for: Actual dollars and how they compare to $150 per hive.
6. Have you ever bought a hive monitor, scale, or other gadget? Tell me about that purchase and how you use it now.
   - Listen for: Willingness to pay for tech, and whether past devices are still in use or abandoned.
7. How many hives do you run now, and how has that number changed over the last three years?
   - Listen for: Units per customer and churn in hive counts.
