# Market brief: A sock that tracks an infant's heart rate and blood oxygen during sleep and alerts the parents' phones

Research date: 2026-10-27. Prices and competitors change, so check the links before relying on them.

Target customer: New parents of babies up to 18 months old. Geography: United States. Depth: quick.

This brief prepares you for customer interviews. It does not show that anyone will buy.

Verified means the quoted text is on the cited page. Read each quote to judge whether it supports the claim.

- **[Verified]** In the same 2004 review, a physician commentator wrote that, anecdotally, they had yet to have parents who did not stop using an apnea monitor early because of constant false alarms. (S18)
- **[Verified]** The same review reports that 83% of parents who consistently used a home monitor felt more secure for having used it. (S19)
- **[Assumption]** Those home-monitor findings come from older medical apnea monitors, so they show what parents value and dislike but say nothing direct about today's smart socks.
- **[Assumption]** A $300 one-time price would match the market leader's price rather than undercut it, so the new product needs a reason to switch other than price.
- **[Assumption]** The core feature set (pulse, oxygen, phone alerts, base station) is already offered by an FDA-cleared incumbent, so differentiation must come from somewhere else, such as fewer false alarms, comfort, or clinical use.
- **[Assumption]** Selling a device that reports blood oxygen and alerts on low readings would likely require FDA clearance, which is costly and slow.

## 2. Riskiest assumptions

1. **Parents have a problem with existing smart socks (false alarms, fit, price, trust) strong enough to switch to a new brand.**
   - Why it's risky: Owlet already sells an FDA-cleared sock with the same features at the same price, and Masimo has a cleared competitor. Without a clear gap, a new entrant has no reason to be chosen.
   - How to test it: Interview 5-8 parents who have used an Owlet or similar sock and ask them to walk through the last time it alerted them and the last time they considered stopping use.
2. **The product can get FDA clearance for oxygen readings and alerts at a cost and timeline the business can survive.**
   - Why it's risky: Owlet's earlier sock had sales halted in 2021 over a regulatory issue. Clearance needs clinical data, which takes money and time before any revenue.
   - How to test it: Book a call with a medical device regulatory consultant and ask for the likely pathway, study size, and cost range for an infant pulse-oximetry wearable.
3. **Pediatricians will not steer parents away from the product.**
   - Why it's risky: AAP guidance, as quoted in a clinical review, says SIDS prevention is not an acceptable reason for home cardiorespiratory monitoring. If pediatricians discourage it, word of mouth suffers.
   - How to test it: Interview 3-5 pediatricians and ask what they told the last parent who asked them about a smart sock.
4. **Around 25% of new-parent households could afford and would consider a $300 vitals monitor.**
   - Why it's risky: This is a guess with no source. The real share drives the market size more than any other input.
   - How to test it: In interviews, ask parents what baby monitor they actually bought, what it cost, and who paid (registry, gift, HSA/FSA, themselves).

## 3. Interview script

Thanks for making time. I'm learning how new parents handle nights with their baby. There are no right answers, and I'm not selling anything today.

1. Tell me about the last night you got up to check on your baby while they were sleeping. What made you check?
   - Listen for: How often worry drives checking, and whether the worry is about breathing or health versus crying or comfort.
2. What do you use today to keep an eye on your baby at night?
   - Listen for: Video monitor, smart sock, wearable, nothing. Brand names, and what they paid.
3. How did you decide which monitor to get? Walk me through it.
   - Listen for: Who decided, where they researched (friends, reviews, pediatrician, registry), and whether price or FDA clearance came up.
4. Tell me about the last time your monitor alerted you. What happened next?
   - Listen for: Whether alerts were real or false, how they felt, and whether they trusted the device afterward.
5. Have you ever stopped using a baby monitor or turned off its alerts? What happened?
   - Listen for: False alarms, fit or comfort problems, battery or connection issues, or simply growing out of the need.
6. What did your pediatrician say the last time you talked about monitors or sleep safety?
   - Listen for: Whether doctors encourage, discourage, or never mention vitals monitors.
7. How did you pay for the monitor you have now?
   - Listen for: Registry gift, HSA/FSA, self-paid, hand-me-down, and how much it cost.
